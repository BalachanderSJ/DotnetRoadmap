What 30 days can actually change

Thirty days will not manufacture ten years of architecture tenure. A company still has to give you the seat, and the seat is earned again every quarter by decisions other people can live with. What 30 focused days will do, on top of eight years of shipping .NET and Angular, is close the gap that keeps a strong senior engineer sounding like a senior engineer: you leave with the artifacts, the tradeoff language, and a way of using AI that a technical architect is expected to show.

By the evening of day 30 you should be able to do five things without notes. Draw the context and the containers for a system you know. Write an architecture decision record that names options, a recommendation, consequences, and a revisit trigger. Turn a quality hope such as 'it must be fast and secure' into a scenario with a number and a window. Lead a one-hour design review without taking the pen. Say what you will not split into microservices, and why.

The trap in this title
Technical architect here means the person who sets and reviews the technical shape of a product estate: boundaries, data ownership, identity, integration, operability, and standards, and who can still read the .NET and Angular. It is not enterprise architect, and it is not a slide role that has left the code. Solution architect, in many services firms, is the same engineering with a heavier workshop, estimate, and stakeholder document. This roadmap trains both rooms. It does not train a vendor-only pre-sales role.

Who you are in the review

You have eight years of delivery. You have shipped Angular screens and .NET APIs. You have felt a release go wrong. The architect interview fails when that history comes out as a list of frameworks. It lands when it comes out as a decision: the constraint, the option you rejected, the consequence, and the signal that would make you reopen it.

Answer shape that scores as an architect

  1. First sentence: the decision, not the background.
  2. Second beat: the constraint that made it (team size, data ownership, latency, threat, cost driver).
  3. Third beat: the option you rejected and the pain it would have added.
  4. Fourth beat: how you would know the decision is failing. A metric, an incident, or a date.
  5. Stop. If they lean in, give one story from your eight years. If you do not know a limit, name the document or the bill you would open. Invented numbers lose the room.

Score each artifact from 0 to 2

  • Decision: a reader can see what you chose.
  • Tradeoff: you named what you gave up.
  • Operable: a person on call can see how it fails and how it rolls back.
  • Evidence: a scenario, a diagram, or a number you labeled as an assumption or a measurement.
  • Six points out of eight is a hire signal on that artifact. A correct diagram with no rejected option still sounds like a senior developer.

The system you will design all month

Harbor is a B2B wholesale portal. Buyer companies place orders. Internal staff release credit holds and watch fulfillment exceptions. You will not build Harbor as a product this month. You will make the architecture decisions a team of about eight to twelve engineers could build.

Label these numbers as assumptions every time you say them. If a panel changes a number, change the design. Peak is about 40 order submits a second. About 2,000 buyer companies and 15,000 users. The accept path, meaning the order is durable before the API returns, targets p95 under 400 milliseconds. Email and other notices can take a minute. One Azure region until an SLO forces a second region. Card numbers stay at the payment provider. You do not store them. The team is two squads that can still talk every day. A million events a second is a different system. Do not design that system by accident.

The containers you are allowed to use

  • Buyer portal and ops portal: Angular. They may be two apps or two areas. Day 19 forces the choice.
  • A backend for frontend, the BFF: ASP.NET Core. The browser's only API.
  • Harbor application: a modular monolith on ASP.NET Core with modules for Catalog, Ordering, and Billing, until an ADR splits one out.
  • Azure SQL: one database, schema per module, unless a later decision says otherwise.
  • Azure Service Bus: something that must survive a process restart and may be handled twice.
  • A notification worker: Azure Functions, isolated worker, because mail is a different failure domain from the accept path.
  • Microsoft Entra ID for staff. Microsoft Entra External ID for buyers on a new system. Azure AD B2C only if you are extending an estate that already has it.
  • Key Vault and managed identity. No passwords in appsettings, and nothing secret in the Angular environment files. Those files ship to the browser.

How to use AI for 30 days without handing it the decision

AI is a critic and a drafter in this plan. It is not the architect. Cursor, in this repository, is the right tool because it can see your ADR next to your principles. A chat window is the right tool for a mock panel. Copilot is fine for a code sketch inside a depth spike. The failure mode is a fluent document you cannot redraw the next morning.

Rules you do not break

  1. Paste the constraints before the question: team size, scale assumption, one region, no card data, .NET and Angular, existing database if any.
  2. Ask for the strongest objection and the missing quality attribute. Do not ask 'is this a good architecture'.
  3. You write the decision sentence yourself. The model may propose options you then accept or reject.
  4. Throw away invented prices, quotas, latency numbers, and legal claims. If a number matters, label it assumption or name the calculator, the bill, or the official limit.
  5. Do not paste secrets, production connection strings, personal data, or unreleased financials. Use a company-approved tenant when the material is work material.
  6. Redraw from memory after every AI session. If you cannot, the model owns the design and you do not.
  7. Check any large sample for a license you do not have. Prefer your own Harbor names over a pasted textbook diagram.

Study material

A senior full-stack developer owns a change through code, tests, and a pull request. A tech lead owns that plus the team's plan: review quality, the release, mentoring, and the design of the service the team ships. A technical architect owns decisions that more than one team will live with for a year or more: where the boundaries are, who owns the data, how identity works, which qualities you will actually pay for, and which standards are written down. You still read code. You stop being the fastest person to take every story.

A solution architect, especially in a services firm, spends more of the week in a workshop and a written proposal: scope, integrations, assumptions, risks, and a shape the client can fund. The engineering underneath is the same. If you cannot explain the failure mode, you are writing a brochure. An enterprise architect sets principles across many products and rarely designs one accept path. That is a later career, not day 30.

Decision rights are the part people skip. You recommend. You do not secretly own the budget, the residual security risk, or the business priority. For Harbor, a typical split is: you recommend modular monolith over microservices; the engineering manager accepts the staffing consequence; security accepts the residual risk of one database; the product owner accepts that email is not in the 400 millisecond budget. Write the names of the roles even if you do not yet have the people. A decision with no owner is a slide.

Influence without authority is the job on the weeks you do not manage the team. You earn it with a short document, a real alternative, and time in the other team's problem. You lose it by announcing a target architecture in week one, by reviewing style instead of boundaries, and by using AI output you cannot explain.

Answer. The tech lead owns one team's delivery: design of their service, review, release, and people. Study material

A quality attribute is a property the system must have, expressed so you can argue about it. 'Scalable' is not one. A scenario is. The form used at the Software Engineering Institute is: source, stimulus, artifact, environment, response, response measure. You do not need the jargon in the room. You do need all six parts on the page.

Source: who or what starts it. Stimulus: the event. Artifact: the part of the system that must respond. Environment: normal load, peak, or already failing. Response: what the system does. Response measure: a number, a window, and how you would observe it.

Harbor accept-path example. Source: a buyer user. Stimulus: submits an order. Artifact: the Ordering module. Environment: peak, about 40 submits a second, assumption. Response: the order and its outbox row are committed, and the API returns the order id. Response measure: p95 under 400 milliseconds over a calendar month, excluding the payment provider's time if payment is asynchronous. If you include the provider, say so, and do not promise 400 milliseconds for their outage.

Percentiles matter. An average of 200 milliseconds can hide a p95 of two seconds. Availability is a different promise from latency. 99.9 percent of a 30-day month is about 43 minutes of unavailability. 99.99 percent is about 4 minutes. Do not offer 99.99 percent for Harbor in one region with one database. You have not paid for it.

Tradeoffs you should be able to say without a slide. Consistency against availability: if Azure SQL is down, Harbor rejects the submit. You do not accept orders you cannot store. Latency against freshness: the catalog page may be a few minutes old; the stock check on submit may not. Coupling against duplication: a read model copies order status into a search store; that copy can be stale. Cost against resilience: a second region doubles a class of bill and forces a data story you do not have yet. Speed of change against uniformity: two squads in one deployable wait for each other sometimes; two deployables without a platform team wait for incidents instead.

A fitness function is an automated check that an architectural choice still holds. Examples you can actually build later this month: a test that the Catalog project does not reference Ordering infrastructure; a unique index on the idempotency key; an accessibility scan on the order route; a pipeline check that fails a new public HTTP client with no timeout. A fitness function without a decision is just a test. Day 18 comes back to this. Today, name two you would want for Harbor.

ISO/IEC 25010 lists qualities such as reliability, security, performance efficiency, maintainability, and compatibility. Use it as a checklist so you notice the one you ignored. Do not recite the standard in the interview. Speak the scenario.

Study material

The C4 model, from Simon Brown, is four levels of zoom. Context: Harbor and the people and systems outside it. Container: the applications and data stores that run, in the Docker sense of a container only by coincidence. A container here is the Angular app, the BFF, the database, the bus. Component: the major parts inside one container, drawn only when you are discussing that container. Code: classes, which do not belong in an architecture review unless someone asks how a specific module is shaped.

A diagram that does not change a decision is decoration. Every box needs a responsibility you can say in one sentence. Every arrow needs a protocol or a 'why'. If two boxes always deploy together and fail together, they are probably one box at container level.

Harbor context, in sentences. Buyers use Harbor to place wholesale orders. Ops staff use Harbor to manage credit and exceptions. Harbor uses Microsoft Entra to authenticate both, in two different directories or configurations. Harbor uses a payment provider to authorize payment and does not store card numbers. Harbor sends mail through an email provider. A reporting analyst is a person, not a system, until they have a warehouse. If they query SQL directly, draw that arrow and label it 'legacy, painful, explicit'.

Harbor API, the modular monolith. Azure SQL. Azure Service Bus. Notification worker on Functions. Key Vault. The telemetry sink, named as Azure Monitor or Application Insights, not as 'logging'. Study material

An architecture decision record is a short document with a status, a context, a decision, and consequences. Michael Nygard described that shape. The point is not the headings. The point is that the next person can see what you chose, what you refused, and what would make the choice obsolete. A wiki page titled 'Architecture' that is edited forever is not a decision. A decision has a date and a status: proposed, accepted, superseded.

Write options as real alternatives, including the one you reject. 'Microservices versus monolith' is too coarse. For Harbor the options are: one modular monolith with module boundaries enforced in code; a handful of independently deployed services on day one; a monolith with no internal boundaries. The third option is how you get a rewrite in two years. The second option is how a team of ten spends the half on pipelines, distributed debugging, and data that no longer shares a transaction.

Consequences are both good and bad. Good: one deployable, one database transaction for an order and its outbox, simpler local development. Bad: a faulty Catalog change can still take down Ordering if you share a process. You mitigate that with module boundaries, tests, and a separate worker for mail, not with a speech about discipline. Also bad: you cannot scale the catalog read path by giving it its own replica count. You accept that until measurement says the accept path or the catalog is the saturated part.

A revisit trigger is mandatory. Examples: a module must be owned by a team that cannot share the release train; a measured hotspot needs independent scale; a compliance boundary forbids shared hosting; the process fails its SLO because of a neighbor module and isolation inside the process is not enough. 'When we feel ready' is not a trigger.

Reference ADR, read only after you draft yours

ADR-001: Harbor ships as a modular monolith
Status: Accepted
Date: (today)

Context:
Two squads, about 8 to 12 engineers, one product, one Azure region.
Peak submit is assumed at about 40 requests a second.
Ordering and the outbox row must commit together.
We have no platform team that runs Kubernetes.

Decision:
One ASP.NET Core deployable with modules Catalog, Ordering, and Billing.
Each module owns its schema. Code may not query another module's tables.
Mail leaves the process through Service Bus to a Functions worker.
We do not create independently deployed business services yet.

Options rejected:

Study material

A bounded context is a boundary inside which a word means one thing and a model is consistent. In Harbor, 'order' in Ordering means the commercial commitment: buyer, lines, price as copied at submit time, status. 'Order' in the warehouse's language might mean a pick list. If you use one class for both, you will leak warehouse fields into the buyer API. Catalog owns products and list prices. That authorization rule lives next to the order.

A context map describes the relationship between contexts. Shared kernel: a tiny model both sides change together. Dangerous if it grows. Customer-supplier: Ordering needs something from Catalog and can ask for a change. Conformist: you take an external model as-is, which is how you should treat the payment provider. Conway's law is the observation that the system copies the communication structure of the organization. Use it as a design input, not a joke. If you create four services and one team, you have given one team four operational surfaces. If you create two squads and forbid them to speak, a modular monolith will still rot at the boundary. Team Topologies gives you four team types. Stream-aligned teams own a flow of work, here the buyer journey and the ops journey, or one team per journey if you split later. A platform team provides a paved path: pipeline, observability, a service template. An enabling team teaches a skill, such as threat modeling, and then leaves. A complicated-subsystem team owns a rare specialty. Harbor does not have that problem yet. Do not invent a platform department for twelve people. A thin shared pipeline owned by a rotating pair is the honest platform.

Interaction modes: collaboration is two teams working together for a defined period, expensive and sometimes necessary at a new boundary. X-as-a-service is a clear API with a small waiting time. Facilitating is the enabling team. Harbor's two squads collaborate on the monolith's boundary rules, then treat modules as an internal contract. They are not two companies.

Cognitive load is the reason you still draw boundaries inside one deployable. A person should be able to change Catalog without holding the Ordering state machine in their head. It is a dependency with a different rule: visual consistency, no business decisions inside a button.

Study material

The browser is an untrusted client. Anything you ship in JavaScript can be read by a script that should not be there, and anything the buyer posts can be rewritten. Prices, roles, and tenant ids in the request body are hints at most. The server recomputes the price from Catalog data, takes the tenant from the authenticated session, and ignores a client-supplied role.

For a first-party Angular app, the default architecture in this plan is a backend for frontend. The Angular app calls only the BFF, same site. The BFF runs the OpenID Connect authorization code flow with PKCE against Entra, keeps the tokens on the server, and gives the browser an HttpOnly, Secure, SameSite cookie. The Angular app does not see the access token, so it cannot put one in localStorage. That removes a common theft path. The cost is a BFF you must operate, and you must handle CSRF because the browser will send the cookie.

CSRF: SameSite=Lax or Strict stops many cross-site posts. For cookie-authenticated writes, also use an anti-forgery token. CORS is not your authorization model. CORS decides which browser origins may read a response. A non-browser client ignores it. Do not describe CORS as 'locking the API'.

The pure SPA alternative is still valid: authorization code plus PKCE in the browser, tokens in memory, refresh done carefully, nothing in localStorage. Use it when a BFF is truly unjustified, for example a temporary app or a host that cannot run the cookie site. Say the threat you accepted: any XSS can act as the user while the token is in memory, and a mistake in storage becomes a lasting theft. The BFF moves that bar to the cookie and the BFF's own session store.

Downstream, prefer the BFF or the monolith to reach Azure SQL and Service Bus with managed identity. Do not forward the user's raw token to every internal API by habit. For Harbor, the monolith enforces authorization itself using the user id, the tenant, and the roles the BFF established. Internal calls use the app's identity. Two app registrations, two audiences, and the ops app is not deployed to the public buyer host if you can avoid it. A buyer token must fail on ops endpoints even if someone copies the URL.

The BFF is not a place to hide business rules. It authenticates, applies a few client-specific shapes, and calls the monolith. If price calculation starts living in the BFF, you have two Ordering models. Aggregation of a read screen is fair. A decision about whether an order is valid is not.

Study material

There are four classic ways two parts of a business exchange data. A shared database, which couples them forever and is the legacy you will meet on day 23. A file drop, which is still how some warehouses work and is acceptable when the latency is hours and the contract is a documented file. A remote call, HTTP or gRPC, where the caller waits. A message, where the caller records the fact and someone else reacts. Pick the style from who is waiting and who owns the data, not from a preference for events.

Inside the Harbor modular monolith, Catalog and Ordering talking in-process is a method call, not a network call. Do not put HTTP between two modules that deploy together just so the diagram looks like services. That pays for failure modes you do not get independence in return for. The method is still a boundary: Ordering calls a public method on Catalog, or reads a price list Catalog published into Ordering's schema on purpose. It does not join Catalog tables.

Use a synchronous call when a person is waiting and you need the answer to finish their action. Harbor's payment authorization is the example, and only if the product insists the buyer sees 'paid' before the screen returns. Then the payment call has a timeout that is part of the architecture, not a default of 100 seconds buried in HttpClient. If the provider is slower than the page should be, change the product shape: accept the order as payment pending, return 201, and let a worker finish the authorization. Do not hold an ASP.NET request open to look reliable.

Use a message when the work can finish after the user has a response, or when the receiver is down and the fact must not be lost. Mail, search indexing, and a warehouse pick list are messages. Azure Service Bus is the broker in this plan because you want a durable queue, a dead-letter queue, and competing consumers. A message is at least once. Duplicate detection on Service Bus is a time window, not a business guarantee that the mail sends once. The consumer must be safe to run twice.

A contract is part of the architecture. For HTTP, OpenAPI is the contract, checked so a breaking change fails CI. For messages, a schema with a version field is the contract. Add fields. Do not change the meaning of an existing field. Do not use a topic as a shared database that every service queries by replaying from the dawn of time unless you have actually designed for that. Harbor does not need event sourcing to ship.

Study material

Data ownership is the architecture. Service boundaries that do not match write ownership are fiction. In Harbor one Azure SQL database is acceptable because ADR-001 kept one deployable. Ownership is still split by schema: catalog, ordering, billing. Application code in Catalog does not query ordering tables. A report that needs both is an explicit read model or a reporting schema fed by a copy, not an ad-hoc join hidden in a controller.

The transaction boundary is the module's own change. Ordering can insert the order row and the outbox row in one transaction. Ordering cannot, in the same transaction, update a table Billing owns and a table in an external warehouse. If you need that, you have a saga, which is day 10, or you have the wrong boundary and the two facts are actually one module.

The transactional outbox solves a specific failure: the order commits, the process dies, the message never leaves. You insert the message payload into an outbox table in the same commit as the order. A relay reads unpublished rows and sends them to Service Bus, then marks them published. The relay can crash after the send and before the mark, so the message can appear twice. That is allowed. Consumers dedupe. Do not 'solve' this by publishing to the bus before the commit. A crash then announces an order that does not exist.

Price is copied onto the order line at submit. Catalog can change the list price a minute later. The order keeps the price the buyer accepted. That copy is deliberate duplication. The alternative, reading Catalog live for every historical invoice, rewrites history.

Multi-tenant means every buyer company is a tenant. The tenant id comes from the authenticated session, never from a hidden field the client can edit. Every order query includes the tenant. A cross-tenant test tries to read company B's order with company A's session and must fail. SQL row-level security can be a backstop so a missed filter still returns nothing. It is not a substitute for the filter you can see in code review. A unique idempotency key is unique per tenant, not globally, unless you designed global keys on purpose.

CQRS means the write model and the read model are different shapes. Use it when the screen fights the transaction. Order history search across a year, with free text, does not belong on the orders table as a pile of LIKE queries. A search index fed by the outbox is a read model. Day 10 — Consistency without fairy tales

Outcome: you can draw the order states, say what happens when a message is delivered twice or out of order, and choose orchestration or choreography for one Harbor saga.

Time box (4 hours)

  • 70 min: duplicates, ordering, sagas, and CAP below.
  • 40 min: Kleppmann chapter 8, the failure modes, skim. Or Hard Parts on sagas.
  • 70 min: state machine and one compensation.
  • 30 min speak, 30 min AI, 10 min redraw the states.

Study material

Distributed systems fail in boring ways: the message arrives twice, the acknowledgement is lost, the receiver is slow, clocks disagree, a process dies between two writes. Harbor does not need a theorem to respect that. It needs a state machine and handlers that can run twice.

At-least-once delivery is the contract you should design for on Service Bus with peek-lock. The lock expires, another worker gets the message, both might have done the work if the first one finished and then failed to complete the message. Exactly-once is a phrase vendors use for a narrow mechanism. The effect you want, one mail and one invoice, is something you build with an idempotency key or a processed-event table. Say that plainly. Duplicate detection on the broker reduces accidents inside a window. It is not the design.

Ordering is not free. If two events for the same order must be applied in order, use Service Bus sessions keyed by order id, and accept less parallelism for that order. If the events commute, or if each event carries the full new status, you do not need sessions. A 'set status to Accepted' event that arrives after 'set status to Cancelled' is a bug unless the handler ignores a stale status. Prefer a state machine that refuses illegal transitions over a hope that the bus stays ordered.

Harbor order states you can defend: Draft is optional and local to the UI. Submitted means we have a durable row. Accepted means it passed credit and any reservation you decided was in the transaction. Rejected means credit or validation failed and the row exists so support can see it. Cancelled means an accepted order was undone by a deliberate compensation. Do not delete the row. Deleting destroys the audit and breaks idempotency.

A saga is a sequence of local transactions with a compensation or a retry when a later step fails. Two styles. Orchestration: one component, here Ordering, tells the next step what to do and stores the saga state. Choreography: each module reacts to events and emits the next. Orchestration is easier to see for two or three steps and one team. Choreography spreads the process across teams and makes the overall state harder to find. For Harbor, if inventory reservation is a second step outside the order transaction, orchestrate it from Ordering. If reservation fails, compensate: mark the order Rejected or Cancelled, release anything you held, and do not send the buyer a success mail. If Billing only needs to hear that an order was accepted, that is choreography of a notification, not a saga. A reaction with no compensator is not a saga. It is a subscriber.

CAP, said carefully: when a network partition happens, a distributed data system cannot give you both perfect availability and perfect linear consistency. It is not a daily slogan and it is not 'you can only pick two of consistency, availability, and partition tolerance' as a product feature matrix. Partitions happen. Harbor's order write chooses consistency: if the database cannot be reached, the submit fails. The catalog read might choose a stale replica and be more available. Say which path chose which. Do not say 'we are AP' for the whole company.

  if not: set Rejected, release nothing that was not held, outbox OrderRejected

  on OrderAccepted, send once per (order id, Accepted)

Study material

STRIDE is a checklist, not a ceremony. Spoofing: someone pretends to be a buyer. Tampering: they change the payload, especially price, quantity of a sku they should not see, or tenant id. Repudiation: a buyer denies they placed the order, and you have no durable record of who was on the session. Information disclosure: company A reads company B's order, or a log line prints a personal email into a shared sink without retention rules. Denial of service: a script submits huge carts or hammers the route. Elevation of privilege: a buyer token calls an ops endpoint, or a viewer role updates an order.

The OWASP API list changes editions. The item you must be able to discuss by its meaning is broken object level authorization. The call is GET /orders/{id}. You load the order by id and return it. The attacker uses their own valid token and someone else's id. The fix is the tenant and the user scope on every read and write, tested with a cross-tenant case. Hiding the id in a GUI is not the fix. A 404 for both 'missing' and 'not yours' avoids confirming that the other id exists.

Identity zones. Staff authenticate against the workforce Entra tenant. Buyers on a new system authenticate against Entra External ID. Existing products may still be on Azure AD B2C. Secrets. Managed identity for Azure SQL, Service Bus, and Key Vault. The Angular environment file is public. It may contain a public client id. Study material

First-party Angular and a public partner do not get the same versioning story. The Angular app ships with the API. You can evolve with expand and contract: add a field, deploy the reader, deploy the writer, remove the old field later. You do not need /v1 in the URL until a client you do not deploy must keep working. When that partner exists, a URL version is the blunt tool they can actually use. Header versioning is easier to get wrong in caches and gateways. Pick URL versioning for the partner API and write it down.

Idempotency on create is an architecture decision because the buyer will double-click and the BFF will retry a lost response. The client sends an Idempotency-Key. Ordering stores it, unique per tenant, with the request hash and the order id. The same key and the same body return the same order and the same status. The same key and a different body return 409. A retry is not a second order. This is the same idea as the outbox consumer, applied to HTTP.

Status codes are a vocabulary. 201 when the order is stored, body includes the id. 400 when the input is invalid. 401 when there is no session. 403 when the session is real but the action is not allowed. 404 when the order is missing or not in this tenant. 409 for an idempotency conflict or an illegal state change. 429 when the rate limit trips. 503 when a dependency you chose to surface is down, with a retry hint only if the client can retry safely. Do not return 200 with an error field for these cases. The Angular client will treat 200 as success.

Error bodies use Problem Details, the shape ASP.NET Core already produces. A type, a title, a status, and a trace id the support person can paste. Do not return a stack trace. Do not return a SQL error. Validation errors can list fields. A payment decline is a domain result the UI can explain, and you decide whether it is 422 or a 201 with a Rejected order. Pick one. The rejected-order choice keeps an audit row. The 422 choice is simpler and easier to lose. Harbor keeps the row. Say so.

Pagination: offset pages lie when new orders insert above the cursor the user is walking, and large offsets get slow. A continuation token or a keyset on (created time, id) is the adult choice for an order list. Cap the page size. Sorting by an unindexed column is a performance incident waiting for a client.

OpenAPI is generated from the API and compared in CI. A breaking change is a failed check, or an explicit ADR if you truly must break a partner. The Angular data-access library is generated or handwritten against that contract, not against a controller you happened to read.

Day 15 — Promises you can burn

Outcome: you can write an SLO for Harbor's accept path, and you can say where retries help and where they make an incident worse.

Time box (4 hours)

  • 70 min: SLI, SLO, error budget, and stability patterns below.
  • 40 min: the SLO chapter in Google's SRE book, or Release It! on timeouts and retries.
  • 70 min: Harbor SLO sheet.
  • 30 min speak, 30 min AI, 10 min restate the error budget in minutes.

Study material

An SLI is the measurement. An SLO is the target you promise for that measurement over a window. An error budget is the amount of failure the SLO allows. For availability, 99.9 percent over 30 days leaves about 43 minutes. That number is 30 days times 24 hours times 60 minutes times 0.001. If you cannot compute it on a whiteboard, do not quote the nines. 99.95 percent is about 22 minutes. 99.99 percent is about 4 minutes. Harbor's accept path, one region, one database, is a 99.9 conversation if the business needs a number at all. It is not a 99.99 conversation.

Separate the indicators. Availability of POST /orders: the fraction of requests that were not server failures, defined carefully so a 400 validation error is not downtime. Latency: p95 and maybe p99 of successful submits. Correctness: duplicate orders per thousand submits, which your idempotency key should drive toward none. Freshness of mail is a different SLO, looser, because you already decided mail is off the request. Do not average them into one green light.

An error budget policy is a human rule. When the budget is gone, you stop shipping changes that add risk, and you spend the time on the reliability work that burned it. Without a person who can say no, the policy is a paragraph. The architect writes the policy. The engineering manager enforces it. Name both.

Timeouts are how you stop waiting forever. Every outbound HTTP call has one. The timeout is lower than the user-facing budget if the call is on the request. Retries are how you survive a blip. Retry only when the operation is idempotent or you have a key that makes it so. Use a small number of tries and jitter so all callers do not stampede back at the same millisecond. Never retry a payment authorization because the response timed out. You do not know if the provider took the money. You reconcile. A circuit breaker opens when a dependency is failing, so you fail fast instead of filling the server with waiting requests. A bulkhead keeps a noisy dependency from using every thread. In Harbor the mail worker is already a bulkhead because it is another process. Inside the API, the payment client gets its own limit if payment stays on the request.

Polly, and the .NET HTTP resilience handler, are the tools. The interview does not need the pipeline syntax. It needs the policy: timeout, retry with jitter for idempotent GETs, no blind retry for payment, circuit breaker around a provider that is already returning errors.

The Angular side of the same decision: the submit button disables after the first click, the Idempotency-Key is generated once per attempt, and a failed read of the order list can show the last list with the time it was fetched. Do not clear the cart on a timeout until you know whether the 201 happened. Offer 'check latest orders' when the response is lost. That is the user-facing half of idempotency.

Day 16 — See the request from the browser to SQL

Outcome: you can say what you trace, what you count, what you log, and which three conditions page a human.

  • 30 min: ASP.NET Core OpenTelemetry overview, skim.
  • 80 min: observability map for submit order.
  • 30 min speak, 30 min AI, 10 min list the three pages from memory.

Study material

Logs, metrics, and traces are three different questions. A log is a discrete event you can read, such as 'order 123 accepted' with a trace id, not a novel. A metric is a number you aggregate over time: request count, error count, latency histogram, CPU. A trace is the path of one request across BFF, monolith, and SQL, made of spans. You need all three. A trace without metrics does not tell you if the error is rare. A metric without a trace does not tell you why this order was slow.

Cardinality is how you drown the bill and the tool. A metric label may be the route template, the status code, the tenant tier if you have a handful of tiers. A metric label must not be the order id, the user id, or the raw URL. Those belong on a trace or a log, sampled if volume demands it. Log ingestion is a cost driver. Logging the body of every submit will include customer data and a large bill. Log the ids, the decision, and the trace id.

Correlation: ASP.NET Core can emit OpenTelemetry traces and metrics, exported OTLP to Azure Monitor or another backend you already operate. W3C traceparent is the header that ties spans together. From the browser, you have two honest options. A real user-monitoring tool starts a trace in the page and propagates traceparent to the BFF. Or the BFF starts the trace and returns a request id header the UI can show in an error dialog. The second is enough for Harbor. Do not claim the Angular app is fully traced if you have not built the first.

The RED signals for the API are rate, errors, and duration. The USE signals for the App Service plan and SQL are utilization, saturation, and errors. Google's four golden signals are latency, traffic, errors, and saturation. You do not need three dashboards that say the same thing. You need one page a tired person can read: submits per minute, error ratio, p95, SQL CPU or DTU saturation, Service Bus dead-letter depth, outbox lag.

Page a human when the SLO is burning fast, when the payment provider or SQL is failing the accept path, or when the dead-letter depth or outbox lag crosses a line you set. Do not page on a single 500. Do not page on mail delay inside its looser SLO. The alert should link to the dashboard and say what 'good' looks like. An alert with no owner is a log.

I page on SLO burn, on the database or payment path being down, and on dead-letter or outbox lag. I do not page on one exception, and I do not log the request body.

Study material

An evolutionary architecture is one you can change with a guided set of checks. A fitness function is that check, automated where you can. For Harbor's future modular monolith, a fitness function is a test that Catalog does not reference Ordering's infrastructure project. NetArchTest is one way to write it. A contract test that the order message still has the fields mail needs is another. A pipeline check that new HTTP clients set a timeout is another. You do not need twenty. The legacy you should be able to discuss looks like this. The business cannot stop taking orders. A rewrite-in-a-room for nine months is how this fails. A strangler moves one slice of behavior to the new system, leaves the rest, and routes only that slice.

Pick a slice that earns its keep and has an edge you can intercept. Example: new orders from the buyer portal go to the new Ordering module, while invoices remain on the old app. The old UI gets a link, or the new BFF routes that path. You do not migrate 'the customer entity' in the abstract. You migrate a journey.

An anti-corruption layer translates the old row into the new model at the edge, in one direction, until the old writer is dead. Old column names do not enter the new domain. If the old and new systems both write the same order row, you do not have a slice. You have a race. During the overlap, only one writer is allowed. The other reads.

Expand and contract is how columns change. Add the new column. Deploy code that can read it. Deploy code that writes it. Backfill. Switch readers. Remove the old column in a later release. Dropping the column in the same release as the new code is how you remove rollback.

The reporting analyst is a stakeholder, not an obstacle you route around. If they query dbo directly, they are a second consumer of the schema. You either support a view with a contract, or you give them a copy, and you do not drop their columns until they have moved. Put that in the plan or the plan is fiction.

Rollback: a feature flag or a route sends submits back to the old writer, which still works because you did not drop its columns and you did not let the new system change a row the old system cannot read. A flag that lives for a year is a permanent branch. Give it an expiry in the ADR. Data written only by the new system during the trial must be readable by the old system or explicitly in scope to discard. Say which.

Do not introduce NgRx, or any global store, until you can name a bug that local state cannot fix, usually distant screens sharing a long workflow and going stale. A store is a cost in boilerplate and in concepts the next developer must learn.

Microfrontends, including module federation or native federation, let two teams release UI independently. They also split the design system, the auth story, the performance budget, and the local developer experience. Choose them when two release trains cannot share a repository and that pain is already here. Harbor's two squads can share a repo. The ADR says no. The revisit trigger is a team that must ship a portal area on a cadence the others block, and a platform person who will own the shell.

Performance is a budget, not a Lighthouse screenshot on your laptop. Set a target you will measure for the buyer home: transferred JavaScript on first load, and largest contentful paint on a throttled profile. An example target you may write as a target, not as a law: a shell that stays under a couple of hundred kilobytes compressed, then measure and adjust. Lazy-load ordering and billing. Do not lazy-load so aggressively that the submit path pays the delay. Server-side rendering helps a public catalog that must paint fast or be indexed. The authenticated order flow can stay client-rendered. Do not SSR the whole authenticated app as a slogan.

Accessibility is a quality attribute. WCAG 2.2 AA is the usual bar. The fitness function is an automated scan on the order path in CI, plus one keyboard pass a human still does on the submit flow. A button that only works with a mouse is a failed accept path for a class of users, not a polish item for later.

Zone.js and zoneless change detection: many production apps still run with Zone.js. Zoneless is a migration you schedule when change detection cost shows up in a profile, or when you are starting a new app and the current Angular documentation marks the path as supported for your major. Check angular.dev the week you write the ADR. Do not make zoneless the headline of your architecture review.

Signals for local state, RxJS at the HTTP edge, no global store until we can name the stale-state bug. No microfrontends while two squads can share a repo. A performance budget and an accessibility check are fitness functions, not slides.

Study material

Governance fails in two directions. A committee that must approve every library, which teaches people to route around you. An absence of decisions, which teaches every squad to pick a different serializer, a different auth library, and a different logging shape. The architect's forum is a short meeting on a fixed rhythm, one hour every two weeks, with a written page in advance. It is not a gate on the sprint.

Bring a topic when it matches a trigger: a new integration or a new data store; a new API that another team or a partner will call; an exception to a written principle; a step-change in cost; a new identity boundary; a production incident that was an architecture miss. Do not bring formatting, a single component, or a NuGet update that follows the existing standard. Those live in code review with the tech lead.

Definition of ready for the forum, one page: the context, at least two options, a recommendation, the quality scenario you are serving, the risk, and the revisit trigger. If the page is missing, the forum does not invent the design live. You send it back. That feels strict and it saves the hour.

A technology radar, in the Thoughtworks sense, has four rings. Adopt: the default, use it. Trial: use it in one place and learn. Assess: study it, do not put it in production yet. Hold: do not start new work with it. Harbor's radar should have about fifteen blips, not a catalog of the industry. Examples you should edit into your own voice. Adopt: modular monolith, BFF with cookie session, outbox, OpenAPI check in CI, managed identity. Trial: .NET Aspire for local orchestration. Assess: microfrontends, multi-region active-passive. The four Accelerate measures tell you whether the governance is hurting delivery. If lead time explodes after you introduce the forum, the forum is too heavy. If change fail rate is high because nobody reviews boundaries, the forum is too light or the fitness functions are missing. You do not need a metrics program this month. You need to know the four names and what a bad movement would mean.

Standards you write: principles, ADRs, API error shape, auth, logging. Standards you do not pretend are architecture: brace style, which an analyzer owns. Spending political capital on braces is how architects become irrelevant while the data model rots.

Day 24 — Case: mail, audit, and search are not one database

Outcome: you can separate three needs that teams often smash into SQL Server or into 'the logs'.

The brief

After submit, Harbor must email the buyer, keep an audit that can answer 'who accepted this order' six years from now, and let ops search last year's orders by company name and sku. A teammate proposes: write it all to the application log, and add three more indexes to the orders table for search. Another proposes event sourcing the entire order history because they read a blog. You have Azure SQL, Service Bus, and the option of a search index if you can justify it.

Study material

Mail is a notification. It can fail, it can be retried, it can be late. It is the outbox and the worker you already designed. It is not the source of truth. If the mail says Accepted and the database says Rejected, the database wins, and you have a bug in the worker to fix.

Audit is a business record. It answers who did what to which order, when, from which tenant. It must survive a log-retention policy that deletes debug logs after 30 days. Store it on purpose: a table Ordering appends to in the same transaction as the state change, or a dedicated audit store with a retention rule the business signed. Six years is a business requirement. Write it down and ask legal or the director if you invented the number. Do not invent six years in the interview if they did not say it. The brief said six. In real life you ask. The audit row is not an application insight log. Different access control, different retention, different reason.

Search is a read model. The orders table serves the transaction: get by id, enforce uniqueness, keep the state machine honest. Searching a year of text across company name and sku with a pile of indexes will fight the writes. A copy, filled from the outbox, can sit in a search index or in a table shaped for the query. It can be seconds or minutes behind. Ops must see a 'as of' time, or they will argue with a buyer about a phantom status. The search store is not a writer of the order. If someone 'fixes' a row only in search, the next event will overwrite it, or worse, you now have two writers.

Event sourcing replaces the order row with a fold of every event. It is justified when you truly need to reconstruct past states as a product feature and you accept the operational cost. Harbor needs the current state, an audit trail, and a search copy. That is an order row plus an audit append plus a projection. Selling full event sourcing here is a different system. Say no, and say what you would revisit: a requirement to answer arbitrary historical questions that an audit table cannot, measured, not imagined.

Reuse Ordering, the idempotency store, the tenant rules, and the outbox. Do not reuse the cookie session. A partner uses an application identity: client credentials or a similar OAuth client flow against Entra, their own app registration, a credential they can rotate. Authorization says which tenant that client may act for. One client must not submit for every tenant because it knows an id. That mapping is server-side.

This is the moment API Management, or a carefully limited gateway, earns a box. You want a key or a token you can revoke, a quota so a bug in their ERP does not consume the accept path, and a versioned contract. The BFF stays the browser's door. If you route the partner through the BFF's cookie endpoints, you will invent a service account password in a hurry. Write the no.

Version the partner contract with a URL version from the start, because you cannot deploy their ERP. Expand and contract still applies inside a version. Give them Problem Details and a request id. Rate-limit with 429 and a documented retry they can honor. Their retries must send the same idempotency key or they will duplicate orders and blame you.

The Angular app does not get a special bypass around the new rules. If both doors write orders, they write through the same Ordering module. Two doors, one writer.

Say this
The partner gets their own client credentials, a tenant mapping we control, a versioned contract, and a quota. They use the same Ordering module and the same idempotency rules. They do not get a browser cookie, and the BFF does not grow a back door for them. API Management is justified now because a partner exists.

Appendix C — Rapid answers

Speak the answer, then stop. Add the lead sentence only if they ask why.

Q1. What is your default for a new .NET and Angular product with one team?

Answer. A modular monolith for the domain, a BFF in front of the Angular app, and a separate worker only for work that must fail independently, such as mail. One database with schema ownership. Managed identity. One region until the SLO and the business say otherwise.

Say this as an architect
I write the revisit trigger at the same time, or the default becomes a religion.

Q2. When do you split a service out?

Answer. When a team must release on its own cadence, when a measured hotspot cannot be fixed inside the process, or when a compliance boundary forbids sharing the host. Not because the diagram has too few boxes.

Say this as an architect
Mail can be a worker earlier, because the failure domain is already different and the user is not waiting.

Q3. How do you handle a double submit?

Answer. An idempotency key, unique per tenant, stored with the order. The same key and body return the same order. The same key and a different body return 409. The UI generates the key once per attempt.

Say this as an architect
The outbox consumer uses the same idea for messages, because the broker is at least once.

Q4. Where does authorization live?

Answer. On the server, on every read and write, using the tenant and the user from the session or token. The Angular app can hide a button. That is not the control. A cross-tenant test is part of the definition of done.

Say this as an architect
I use 404 when the id is outside the tenant so we do not confirm it exists.

Q5. What do you put in the browser?

Answer. An HttpOnly Secure cookie issued by the BFF, plus an anti-forgery token on writes. Not the access token, and not a secret in the Angular environment file.

Say this as an architect
If we skip the BFF, the token stays in memory and I state the XSS risk instead of pretending localStorage is fine.

Q6. How do you choose between App Service, Functions, Container Apps, and AKS?

Answer. App Service for the request API, Functions for the event worker, Container Apps when we need containers without operating a cluster, AKS when a platform team already runs one and the workload needs it. Harbor does not start on AKS.

Say this as an architect
Every choice is a pager. I can say who answers it.

Q7. What is your availability target?

Answer. I do not pick nines from a hat. For a single-region Harbor I would discuss 99.9 percent on the accept path, about 43 minutes a month, as a starting SLO, and I would label it an assumption until we measure. I would not offer 99.99 percent without a second region and a data strategy.

Say this as an architect
Mail is a different, looser SLO. I do not hide it inside the accept number.

Q8. What is a fitness function?

Answer. An automated check that an architecture decision still holds. A test that one module cannot reference another's tables, a contract check on the order event, a timeout required on new HTTP clients, an accessibility scan on the submit route.

Say this as an architect
I would rather have two that match real incidents than a dashboard of twenty nobody owns.

Q9. Would you use NgRx?

Answer. Not by default. Signals for feature state, RxJS at the HTTP boundary. A global store when distant parts of a long workflow go stale and we can name that bug. The store is a cost.

Say this as an architect
The larger Angular decision is the boundary between buyer and ops, and between catalog and ordering, not the state library.

Q10. How do you work with AI as an architect?

Answer. I write the decision. The model attacks it: missing quality, weakest objection, fictional numbers. I do not paste secrets, I do not accept a price it invented, and I redraw the design from memory. If I cannot, I do not understand it yet.

Say this as an architect
Cursor is useful when it can see the ADR next to the principles and only report contradictions.

Appendix E — What this month will not make you

You will not be an enterprise architect, a security consultant, or a person who has operated a multi-region system through a real failover. You will not have read Kleppmann or the Hard Parts cover to cover. You will not have a right to a title. You will have a portfolio you can defend, a way of speaking in decisions, and a 90-day plan that does not start with a rewrite.

If a week collapses because of work or family, do not quit the sequence and do not double the hours the next day. Keep the artifact and the spoken answer. Slide the external reading to the weekend or drop it. The calendar is a scaffold. The ADRs are the point.

When you finish day 30, book a conversation with the strongest architect you know and ask them to spend 45 minutes trying to break ADR-001 and the partner API. A human who has carried a pager will find things this document and the model both missed. That conversation is the start of the job, not a graduation.

A last rule about numbers
Every peak, latency, retention, and availability figure in this document is a teaching assumption for Harbor, except the calendar arithmetic on a 30-day month and the published .NET support window. If you say them in a real review, label them. Then replace them with the measurement, the bill, or the official limit.
